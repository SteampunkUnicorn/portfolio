When a Design System Becomes Technical Debt.

Reusable doesn’t always mean maintainable.

Design systems are supposed to make development easier.

They provide reusable components, establish consistent patterns, and help teams deliver interfaces without rebuilding the same solutions again and again. At least, that's the idea.

After more than 15 years building frontend applications and reusable interfaces, I've learned that reusability comes with a cost. Every variation adds a decision. Every exception becomes something to support. And sometimes, a component built to simplify development becomes one of the most complicated parts of the application.

At what point does maintaining the abstraction become harder than maintaining the interfaces?

I'm not arguing against design systems. I'm arguing for systems that actually reduce complexity instead of simply moving it somewhere harder to see.

01 / 10

Reusability isn’t the same as maintainability

Sharing a component seems efficient: improve the implementation once, and every use benefits. Often it works exactly that way.

But reuse only helps when those implementations genuinely share responsibilities. A promotional card, a linked article teaser, and an expandable information panel may look alike while needing very different content models and interactions.

Combining them behind one API can turn a simple implementation into a web of modifiers. Shared code isn't automatically good architecture. If the abstraction obscures what a component actually does, the maintenance cost may be greater than the duplication it prevents.

02 / 10

The problem with the universal component

I've encountered this especially often with components across content-managed websites. A component starts with an image, a heading, a description, and a link. Then one page needs the image on the right. Another needs an optional eyebrow. Another wants a compact layout. Another has unique mobile behavior.

Each request seems too small to justify a separate component. So we add another option. Eventually developers spend more time battling the component's assumptions and overrides than implementing the actual design.

What combinations are supported? Which setting takes precedence? What happens when an editor chooses two options that were never intended to work together? Each variation becomes something to understand, test, and maintain.

Reuse should be based on shared responsibility, not just visual similarity. Two components can share tokens, styles, and primitives without sharing every branch of their implementation.

03 / 10

When the CMS becomes part of the component API

Content management systems amplify that problem. In a standalone application, a developer might choose a component's props directly. In a CMS, those choices become editor-facing controls: a layout dropdown, an optional-field checkbox, an alignment selector, a theme, a media position.

Those decisions affect more than CSS. They become part of the content model, the editing experience, template logic, validation rules, and QA workload. A component that began as a content block can gradually turn into its own miniature page builder.

Small variations become permanent configuration

Consider a promotional feature. Initially, an editor supplies a heading, a description, an image, and a link. Then comes a version without an image; then optional eyebrow text; then alignment, different widths, and selectable backgrounds. Each new field makes the editor interface more complicated and adds combinations that designers and developers need to support.

That flexibility may be appropriate when it solves a deliberate editorial need. It becomes debt when it merely accumulates one-off requests.

Old content makes refactoring harder

Once the component is in use across dozens or hundreds of pages, changing a default can alter existing content. Removing a field may require migrations. Reworking markup may affect client-specific themes, legacy values, and content that no one remembers editing.

Now the technical debt lives in the relationship between frontend code, CMS schema, stored configuration, and existing pages. A quick refactor isn't always quick anymore.

Give flexibility boundaries

Editors need useful choices, not an endless matrix of exceptions. A component should have a clear purpose, with configuration options representing legitimate variations of that purpose. When requirements change its responsibility, a new component can be simpler and safer.

Sometimes the best thing we can give editors is fewer options that behave predictably.

04 / 10

Design tokens don’t solve architectural problems

Tokens are valuable for shared colors, spacing, typography, and visual decisions. Semantic values such as --color-text-muted can communicate intent and support large changes.

But a token system can also accumulate component-specific exceptions and nearly identical values. When every one-off variation becomes a new permanent token, it loses the clarity it was meant to provide.

Tokens can keep distinct components visually consistent. They don't require those components to have the same content model or behavior.

05 / 10

Accessibility is inherited—including the mistakes

Shared components can make accessible behavior dependable. A well-built dialog handles focus; a disclosure communicates its expanded state; a form field connects labels and errors. Every implementation benefits.

Reuse also spreads mistakes. A broken focus pattern or incorrect ARIA attribute can affect every page that uses a component. That's why accessible semantics and keyboard behavior belong in the component's original contract.

A reusable component should make correct behavior easier, not distribute errors more efficiently.

06 / 10

The hidden cost of exceptions

In a CMS, exceptions often become content fields, styling modifiers, and template conditionals. One client needs a special campaign layout. Another needs a different call to action. Another needs a variation that applies only at a particular breakpoint.

Over time, developers can become reluctant to change the base component because the impact is difficult to predict. The system intended to make changes safer now makes them riskier.

Not every unique design deserves a shared modifier. An isolated implementation can be the more maintainable choice—especially if the alternative is making every future use inherit its complexity.

07 / 10

A component library isn’t the whole design system

A component library provides implementations. A design system also needs shared patterns, guidance, accessibility expectations, documentation, and a way to evolve.

Without ownership, a component can become a dependency everybody relies on but nobody feels comfortable changing. Without clear guidance, developers reinvent existing patterns or add more configuration instead of choosing the appropriate component.

Governance doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs to match the team and clarify what belongs in the system, how changes are reviewed, and when something should stay independent.

08 / 10

When abstraction becomes an obstacle

One warning sign is developers working around the system: copying components, overriding styles locally, avoiding upgrades, or building one-offs because the shared component takes longer to configure than the feature itself.

That's worth investigating. Are the APIs unclear? Are documented patterns too rigid? Are they doing too much? The response shouldn't automatically be stricter rules for developers.

A design system should reduce the decisions developers need to make—not become another layer of complexity to fight through.

A design system should reduce the number of decisions developers have to make—not create a new layer of complexity they have to navigate.
09 / 10

Knowing when to refactor, separate, or remove

Technical debt isn't necessarily a sign of bad engineering. Requirements change; products grow; content accumulates. A once-useful abstraction can outgrow its original purpose.

Ask which responsibilities the component now handles, which variations are genuinely in use, what combinations are hard to test, and whether separating components would make changes safer. For CMS implementations, add the migration cost and existing content dependencies to that evaluation.

Sometimes refactoring is right. Sometimes a new focused component is better. Sometimes a stable legacy implementation should be left alone until there's a compelling reason to migrate it.

The aim isn't purity. It's to make future work understandable and manageable.

10 / 10

Design systems should make good decisions easier

A healthy design system makes common, useful patterns easy to build consistently. Developers can trust the underlying behavior. Designers can work within a coherent visual language. Editors can make understandable choices without navigating a maze of configuration.

That doesn't mean supporting every variation. It means setting thoughtful boundaries around what is shared, what is specialized, and how each piece should evolve.

After years of working with component libraries and multi-site architectures, I've come to value clear responsibilities more than maximum reuse. Sometimes two focused components are better than one universal abstraction.

A design system should make good engineering decisions easier. When it starts making them harder, it's time to reconsider the system.
