Not Every Problem Needs AI.

When did adding complexity become synonymous with innovation?

I have spent more than 15 years building websites and interactive applications, watching technologies evolve and development practices change. One principle has stayed consistent: the best solution is not necessarily the most sophisticated one.

Artificial intelligence is an extraordinary technology. But somewhere in the rush to add it to everything, we seem to have forgotten an important engineering question: does this problem actually need AI?

We have spent decades developing reliable algorithms and software architectures. Those approaches have not suddenly become obsolete because a model can perform some of the same tasks.

And when companies start replacing experienced workers with AI, the stakes rise. What happens when we remove the people who understand the systems we are asking AI to maintain?

Sometimes AI is transformative. Other times it is an expensive and unpredictable solution to a simple problem. The cost of that distinction may not become apparent until something breaks.

01 / 09

The temptation to make everything intelligent

Whenever a powerful new technology emerges, there is pressure to incorporate it into existing products. Some experimentation is useful; we cannot know what a technology enables without exploring it. But experimenting with AI is different from assuming every workflow would benefit from it.

Consider an email inbox. Sorting by sender, filtering by keywords, and applying explicit rules are problems software has solved reliably for years. If I make a rule to move messages from a particular sender into a folder, I do not need a model to reinterpret my intention every time. I need a condition and an action.

The same goes for calculations, form validation, data transformations, and navigation logic. The question should not be whether AI can solve the problem. It should be whether AI solves it better—and what better actually means for the people using it.

02 / 09

There is value in knowing what your code will do

Deterministic logic provides something easy to overlook: given the same inputs and conditions, it produces the same result. When I validate a form, calculate a price, or enforce a business rule, I usually want the outcome to be unambiguous.

If someone selects three toppings for a pizza, the application should add three toppings. That is not a problem that benefits from interpretation.

Language models can interpret ambiguous language, synthesize information, and produce useful content. Those strengths come with a different failure model: outputs are not inherently guaranteed to satisfy every factual or business constraint. Sometimes they can be convincingly wrong.

Correct-looking code and correct code are not the same thing. That distinction matters when deciding which parts of an application should be probabilistic and which must remain predictable.

03 / 09

Complexity has a cost. So does being wrong.

Every dependency, abstraction, and service adds something to a system. Its value should outweigh its cost. AI integrations deserve the same scrutiny as any other architectural decision.

Model-driven features can introduce latency, usage costs, privacy questions, testing overhead, observability needs, and failure cases that are difficult to detect. A conventional function might throw an error; a model may return an answer that seems plausible but quietly violates a requirement.

Validation, guardrails, fallback behavior, and human review may be necessary. That can be an acceptable tradeoff when AI enables something valuable. It is harder to justify when straightforward logic would have met the original requirement.

Introducing complexity is not inherently innovation. Sometimes it is just more complexity.

04 / 09

What happens when AI replaces the people who know the work?

Companies are increasingly looking to AI to automate work previously handled by experienced employees. There are real opportunities to remove repetitive work. But automating routine tasks is not the same as replacing everything a knowledgeable person contributes.

The customer-service company examples are instructive. Klarna initially championed AI-assisted customer support, then moved to recruit human support staff again amid concerns about service quality. The lesson is not that AI is useless. It is that headline efficiency claims do not necessarily capture the value of expertise, judgment, and exception handling.

Experienced workers know when something is abnormal. They remember why exceptions exist, how customers actually behave, and which apparently minor details carry real consequences. Those contributions can be invisible until the people who provided them are gone.

The apparent savings of replacing expertise may be immediate. The cost of losing context can take longer to emerge.

05 / 09

Generating code is not understanding a codebase

AI coding tools can produce convincing implementations, suggest refactors, generate tests, and speed up repetitive work. I use the distinction between implementation and engineering deliberately: a mature application is more than a set of files and functions.

It contains years of decisions, compromises, business rules, integration constraints, and lessons learned. A seemingly redundant check might protect against a legacy edge case. An awkward conditional might support a workflow that remains crucial to a subset of users.

A model can suggest removing code it regards as unnecessary without knowing why that code exists. Developers can make the same mistake—but that is a reason to preserve and improve engineering understanding, not replace it with unchecked output.

The danger is not just that AI might write bad code. It is that organizations might lose the people who can recognize when apparently good code is wrong.

The code can often tell you what an application does. It does not always tell you why it needs to do it that way.

06 / 09

Someone still has to understand the consequences

Imagine an AI coding agent simplifying an old component: it consolidates repeated logic, removes a condition, and writes tests that pass. But what if that condition supports keyboard focus in an uncommon interaction state, or preserves a client requirement missing from the test suite?

Someone still has to evaluate the change against the actual product. They need to know how the system is used, which assumptions the tests omit, and whether a refactor causes regressions in performance, accessibility, or integrations.

AI can assist with that investigation. It cannot absolve a team of ownership. If a company removes experienced developers and expects a much smaller team to approve a flood of generated changes, it risks turning oversight into a rubber stamp.

Generating code is not the same as maintaining software.

07 / 09

Where AI genuinely earns its place

Some of the strongest AI use cases involve ambiguity, interpretation, or unstructured information. Natural-language requests, document exploration, writing support, and investigating unfamiliar code can all benefit from models.

I also see AI as a valuable productivity tool for developers: it can propose approaches, explain concepts, and reduce repetitive work. But generated code still needs to be understood, tested, and maintained, and architectural suggestions still need to be evaluated against actual constraints.

The most compelling products use models for the work models are good at while retaining deterministic logic for explicit rules, verified data, and required outcomes.

08 / 09

Maybe we are asking the wrong question

Before choosing a technology, ask what the user is trying to accomplish, how much uncertainty the task contains, what happens when the system is wrong, and who will maintain it. Those questions are more useful than asking whether a feature could be labeled AI-powered.

Product search makes a useful example. Filtering by category, availability, and price is structured logic. Interpreting a conversational request from someone who does not know the correct terminology might benefit from AI. The two approaches can work together: a model interprets intent, and conventional application code applies constraints to verified information.

That is the kind of architecture I find compelling: use AI where interpretation creates value and deterministic software where repeatability matters.

09 / 09

Knowing when not to build something

Experience has taught me that good engineering often involves removing unnecessary complexity rather than adding it. Clever implementations are appealing, but software also needs to survive changing requirements, production failures, and other developers maintaining it.

AI will continue to change software development, and that creates exciting possibilities. It does not mean experienced engineers become unnecessary. If anything, automation makes architectural understanding, verification, and judgment more important.

Software engineering has never just been typing code. It is understanding problems, managing tradeoffs, and taking responsibility for systems that other people depend on.

The mark of good engineering is not using the most advanced technology available. It is understanding the problem well enough to choose the right technology in the first place. Sometimes the most innovative decision is knowing when not to add anything at all.
