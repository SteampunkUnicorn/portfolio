Making Performance a Practice.

Turning a technical concern into an engineering discipline—and a sustainable client service.

Modern websites are expected to do a lot.

Large, immersive hero features. Rich photography and video. Interactive components that keep visitors engaged. Third-party integrations that support analytics, marketing, and business objectives.

Each of those features can contribute to a compelling experience. But they also come with a cost.

During my time as a Senior Frontend Developer at Miles Partnership, I saw how increasingly image-heavy content, complex hero sections, and interactive features could introduce significant performance overhead.

These weren't necessarily bad design decisions. They were the kinds of experiences our clients needed. The challenge was delivering them without sacrificing the speed, responsiveness, and stability that make those experiences enjoyable in the first place.

At the same time, the search landscape was changing. Core Web Vitals had become part of Google's broader approach to evaluating page experience, while AI-generated search summaries were introducing new questions about how websites would remain discoverable.

As I followed these developments, I became increasingly interested in how technical performance might affect both the experience of visiting a website and its ability to compete for visibility.

I saw an opportunity to make performance a more deliberate part of our engineering process. That became the foundation for the Core Web Vitals initiative I established at Miles Partnership.

01 / 06

Recognizing the problem

The websites we worked on had to balance competing priorities. They needed to be visually engaging, provide rich content, support interactive functionality, and meet business objectives. But the same features that made them compelling could also introduce performance challenges.

Large hero images could delay the appearance of primary content. Image-heavy pages increased the amount of data browsers needed to download and render. Interactive features could add JavaScript execution costs that affected responsiveness.

Optimizing any individual feature was only part of the solution. The larger challenge was how to keep performance from gradually deteriorating as websites evolved.

New content, functionality, and requirements were always going to be introduced. Performance couldn't depend entirely on occasional cleanup or on whichever developer happened to be working on a particular component. It needed an ongoing process.

02 / 06

Connecting performance to discoverability

My interest in Core Web Vitals wasn't limited to frontend implementation. I was also paying attention to changes in search.

Traditional SEO had long involved technical considerations, but emerging AI-generated search experiences raised new questions about how websites would be found and surfaced. I had been reading about the relationship between page experience, search performance, and the evolving role of AI-generated summaries.

Strong Core Web Vitals alone don't guarantee rankings or inclusion in AI-generated answers. Still, that changing search landscape reinforced something I already believed: technical quality and content discoverability shouldn't be treated as entirely separate concerns.

For client websites that depended on attracting visitors through search, performance was part of maintaining a competitive digital presence. It gave me another reason to advocate for performance as a continuing investment rather than an occasional development task.

03 / 06

From engineering initiative to business strategy

I founded an internal Core Web Vitals initiative to establish a more consistent approach to performance work. My goal was to move beyond identifying and fixing isolated issues and help create a repeatable engineering practice.

I authored the internal Core Web Vitals documentation and took responsibility for developing and reviewing ongoing performance-related tasks. But I also recognized that making the initiative sustainable would require more than a technical process.

I worked closely with a Digital Product Manager to develop a business concept around ongoing performance optimization. Together, we explored how Core Web Vitals work could be structured as a repeatable, sustainable service rather than handled solely through individual development requests.

From there, we worked with the sales team to discuss how to position the offering for clients, communicate its value, and incorporate performance work into ongoing client relationships.

This was an especially rewarding part of the initiative because it required translating engineering concerns into business priorities. For developers, the value of improving loading performance, responsiveness, or visual stability can feel self-evident. For clients, those improvements need to connect to broader goals: providing better user experiences, protecting the quality of their websites, and supporting their digital presence.

04 / 06

Balancing design ambition with engineering realities

One of the things I appreciate most about frontend development is working at the intersection of design, functionality, and technical constraints. Performance work makes those relationships especially visible.

A large hero image might be central to a design concept, but that doesn't mean it needs to become an obstacle to loading the page. An interactive feature might offer genuine value, but its implementation still deserves scrutiny if it introduces excessive processing or delays user interactions.

The goal isn't to eliminate rich experiences. It's to understand their technical cost and make thoughtful decisions about how they're delivered.

Core Web Vitals provide a useful framework for that work by focusing attention on loading performance, responsiveness, and visual stability. But the metrics themselves aren't the end goal. They're signals that help us investigate whether the experience we're building is working well for the people using it.

05 / 06

Making performance part of the business

One of the most meaningful outcomes of the initiative was its incorporation into contracts for multiple clients. What began as an engineering concern had evolved into an ongoing service that could be discussed, planned, and delivered as part of client relationships.

That transition didn't happen through code alone. It required collaboration between engineering, digital product management, and sales, with each perspective contributing to how the initiative could become sustainable.

For me, it demonstrated that technical leadership isn't limited to making architectural decisions or mentoring other developers. It can also mean identifying an opportunity, building relationships across departments, and helping turn a technical priority into something the business can support over time.

The result was a more sustainable foundation for performance work and a way for clients to invest in the continued health of their websites.

06 / 06

What I took away

The biggest lesson I took from establishing the Core Web Vitals initiative is that performance is a practice, not a milestone.

You can optimize an image, reduce JavaScript overhead, or eliminate a layout shift. Those are important improvements. But without ongoing attention, the same issues—or entirely new ones—can appear as a website grows.

Building a sustainable approach means establishing the processes that make performance visible and actionable long after an individual optimization is complete.

The experience also reinforced something I've come to appreciate throughout my career: some of the most valuable engineering contributions happen outside the codebase. Recognizing an opportunity, building consensus, documenting processes, and collaborating across teams can have an impact that extends far beyond a particular implementation.

That's what made this initiative particularly meaningful to me. It combined the technical work I enjoy with the opportunity to influence engineering practices, product strategy, and client relationships. And it's an example of the kind of work I want to continue doing.
