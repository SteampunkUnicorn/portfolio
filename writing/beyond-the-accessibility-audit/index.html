An accessibility audit can tell you where a website is falling short. But by the time a problem appears in an audit, the underlying decisions may already be built into the markup, components, and interactions.

As Lead Accessibility Developer working across nearly 100 websites, I conducted accessibility quality assurance, documented and prioritized findings, wrote remediation tasks, reviewed other developers’ work, and implemented fixes myself.

I also wanted to prevent those issues from being introduced in the first place. I worked closely with developers on accessible markup during feature builds and began an initiative to include accessibility and ARIA checks in every code review.

Accessibility isn’t just about meeting a standard. It’s about understanding what prevents someone from using a website—and making informed engineering decisions to remove those barriers.

01 / 07

Building accessibility into the markup

Many accessibility problems begin with relatively small implementation decisions: images without appropriate text alternatives, form controls without labels, or links whose purpose is unclear. These issues are easy to overlook when accessibility is treated as a late-stage check rather than an everyday development concern.

As Lead Accessibility Developer, I was closely involved in ensuring our markup was accessible as it was written. I emphasized semantic HTML, meaningful accessible names, and ARIA that accurately represented the component’s behavior. Where native HTML offered the right behavior, it was preferable to adding ARIA to compensate for unsuitable markup.

I also began an initiative to make accessible markup and appropriate ARIA usage a regular part of every code review. The aim was to prevent recurring problems instead of repeatedly discovering them at audit time.

02 / 07

Testing beyond the automated report

Our accessibility quality assurance was guided by WCAG 2.1 Level AA. I used Lighthouse, the AQA Chrome extension, VoiceOver, and manual keyboard testing to examine sites from multiple perspectives.

Automated tools were useful for catching detectable issues, but they could not tell the whole story. Keyboard testing exposed navigation and focus behavior. VoiceOver helped reveal whether content and changing interface states were understandable to screen-reader users.

An interface can contain technically valid attributes and still be difficult to use. Testing needed to evaluate the interaction itself, not simply whether an automated check passed.

03 / 07

When interfaces change

Some of the most involved accessibility issues appeared in dynamic interfaces: search results, expandable sections, pop-ups, modal dialogs, and carousels. Visually, a change may be obvious; programmatically, users need to be told what changed and be able to continue navigating.

Search results and live regions. When results update without a page reload, an appropriately designed status announcement can communicate that results are available or that nothing was found. Announcement timing and frequency matter as much as the presence of aria-live.

Expanded and collapsed controls. Toggle controls should use appropriate semantics and keep aria-expanded synchronized with the actual state of the associated content. That state must be conveyed to assistive technology, not only through changing visual icons.

Modal dialogs. Opening a modal requires appropriate dialog semantics, an accessible name, and predictable focus management. Background interaction should be unavailable while a modal dialog is active, and focus should generally return to a logical place on close.

Carousels and active slides. Users need to understand which slide is presented, operate navigation controls, and avoid accidentally tabbing into interactive content on inactive slides. Changes should be announced thoughtfully without creating unnecessary interruptions.

These problems have a common thread: accessibility is part of application state. When a component opens, closes, updates, or becomes active, the semantic and keyboard experience needs to remain consistent with what is visible.

04 / 07

From findings to development priorities

Working across nearly 100 websites meant that similar issues could occur in very different technical contexts. I conducted AQA, documented findings, set accessibility priorities, reviewed other developers’ remediation, and implemented some fixes myself.

Our remediation tasks described the issue, the relevant WCAG 2.1 AA success criterion, why the implementation failed that criterion, and a suggested remediation. This provided a consistent technical starting point for developers.

The suggestion needed to be specific enough to communicate an accessible outcome without assuming intimate knowledge of every site’s architecture or functionality. A solution that works on one component can be inappropriate for another.

I prioritized issues using my judgment about how severely they interfered with someone’s ability to use the site. That judgment was informed by research into the barriers encountered by users with different accessibility needs—not by assuming that all WCAG failures had identical practical impact.

I then passed the priorities to a project manager, who balanced them with task estimates and developer availability to assign work. That distinction mattered: I owned the accessibility assessment and priorities; project management coordinated scheduling and assignments.

05 / 07

Moving accessibility earlier

I wanted accessibility to be considered during feature development and code review, instead of appearing only after an audit identified failures.

By bringing attention to image alternatives, form labels, link purpose, semantic controls, ARIA states, and keyboard behavior as markup was written, the team could address many of the same classes of issues before they became separate remediation tasks.

The goal was not to turn code review into a mechanical checklist. It was to strengthen developers’ understanding of the accessible behaviors their components needed to support.

06 / 07

From remediation to prevention

Using accessible markup during new development and feature builds made a significant difference to the process. AQA results were strong across subsequent testing periods, reinforcing the value of catching problems before a formal audit.

Even more encouraging was the change in how developers approached unrelated work. I began seeing developers make ARIA and accessibility improvements while completing tasks that had not been assigned as accessibility remediation.

That change in behavior was an important outcome. Accessibility was becoming part of developers’ day-to-day engineering decisions, rather than something addressed only through specialized tickets. I cannot share client reports or quantitative results, but that shift in practice was visible in the work itself.

07 / 07

What I took away

Accessibility standards are important, but meaningful accessibility work starts with understanding the barriers users encounter. My research informed how I weighed issues, and hands-on testing helped me evaluate whether a component actually worked across different ways of interacting.

Working across nearly 100 websites taught me to specify the required accessible outcome clearly while leaving room for each implementation’s constraints. Leading the work required technical investigation, documentation, prioritization, direct remediation, review, and collaboration with project management.

The most meaningful progress was not simply seeing strong AQA results. It was seeing developers recognize and address accessibility issues before those issues needed a dedicated task. Accessibility had begun moving from remediation toward prevention.
